# The promise
[img] a lone white humanoid robot seen from behind, sitting at a beige desk with a glowing CRT monitor, endless grid of empty cubicles {move:in}
A few years ago, every company was sold the same promise.

[img] a boardroom at dawn, long table, empty leather chairs, a single robot standing at the window looking out over the city
Fewer people. Faster work. Profits that climb overnight.

[img] a vast open-plan office with every desk empty, one desk lamp still on {move:out}
Then someone actually counted.

[img] a single printed page under a desk lamp, everything around it in shadow {move:in}
[card] 95% | of organisations: no measurable return
Ninety-five percent of the organisations studied got no measurable return from it.
[pause 0.8]

[img same] {move:hold}
Not a loss. Just... nothing. So what went wrong?

# The report
[img] a quiet university library at night, green reading lamps, a robot reading a thick printed report
In 2025, researchers at MIT went looking for the answer.

[img] a long corridor of filing cabinets with hundreds of labelled folders, cool fluorescent light {move:panR}
They studied more than three hundred public AI projects, and talked to the people running them.

[img] a wall of old beige monitors all showing the same flat line graph, dim light
[card] $30–40 billion | spent on generative AI
Companies had spent an estimated thirty to forty billion dollars.

[img] a tiny desk with one small plant, lit by a single window, in a huge dark office
And only about five percent of the projects that went live made real money.

# Why it stalled
[img] a robot at a desk being handed a yellow sticky note by a human hand
Imagine hiring an assistant who forgets everything you taught them, every single morning.

[img same] {move:in}
That, the researchers said, was the real problem.

[img] a robot staring at a CRT screen full of blank forms, papers piled on both sides {move:panL}
The tools didn't learn. They didn't remember feedback, or adapt to how the work is really done.
[pause 0.6]

[img] two desks side by side, one with an official grey terminal switched off, the other with a personal phone glowing
Meanwhile, workers were quietly using their own AI tools.

[img same] {move:in}
[card] 90% vs 40% | workers using AI · companies that bought it
About nine in ten said they did. Only about four in ten companies had bought an official one.

[img] a person at a 1990s desk glancing over their shoulder while typing on a phone under the desk
Think about the last time you used an AI tool at work. Did your company give it to you?
[pause 1.0]

[img] a glossy sales stage with empty seats and a spotlight on an empty podium {move:out}
And the money went to the loud places. More than half of it went to sales and marketing.

[img] a calm back office with neat shelves, a robot sorting invoices under a small warm lamp {move:up}
But the best returns were hiding in the boring back office.

# The five percent
[img] a robot and a human sitting across a desk from each other, both looking at the same CRT screen
So what did the five percent do differently?

[img same] {move:in}
They picked one narrow job. They bought tools that could learn. And they let the people doing the work decide.

[img] a small team around one desk, a robot handing a folder to a woman who is smiling {move:panR}
[card] 2 in 3 | bought tools that worked, vs about 1 in 3 built in-house
Tools bought from specialists worked about two times in three. Tools built in-house, about a third as often.

# The turn
[img] the same endless cubicle grid as the opening, now with a few desks lit and people sitting beside the robots {move:out}
So the takeover didn't fail because AI is useless.

[img same] {move:in}
It stalled because replacing people was the wrong goal.
[pause 0.8]

[img] the lone robot from the opening, now turning its head toward a person sitting down at the next desk {move:hold}
And that might be the best news in this whole story.
