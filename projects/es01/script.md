# Hook
[img] a lone white humanoid robot seen from behind, sitting at a beige desk with a glowing CRT monitor, endless grid of empty cubicles
When AI first walked into the corporate world, it came with a simple promise.

[img] a boardroom at dawn, long table, empty leather chairs, a single robot standing at the window looking out over the city
Fewer people. Faster work. Productivity that climbs overnight.

[img] a vast open-plan office with every desk empty, one desk lamp still on {move:out}
On paper, it made complete sense.

[img same] {move:in}
In practice, something else happened.

# The report
[img] a quiet university library at night, green reading lamps, a robot reading a thick printed report
In 2025, researchers at MIT looked at more than three hundred public AI projects, and interviewed and surveyed the people running them.

[img] a wall of old beige monitors all showing the same flat line graph, dim fluorescent light
Companies had poured somewhere between thirty and forty billion dollars into generative AI.

[img] a single printed page on a desk under a lamp, a large number 95 printed on it, everything else blurred
And ninety-five percent of the organisations they studied were getting no measurable return from it. Not a loss. Just nothing.

# Why
[img] a robot standing in a corridor of filing cabinets, drawers open, papers everywhere {move:panR}
The reason wasn't that the models were bad. It was that most of them couldn't learn.

[img] a robot at a desk being handed the same sticky note again by a human hand
They didn't remember feedback. They didn't adapt to how a company actually works. So every day, people had to explain the same things again.

[img] two desks side by side in a dim office, one with an official grey terminal switched off, the other with a personal phone glowing
Meanwhile, something strange was happening at the desks. Most workers were already using their own AI tools, quietly, while far fewer companies had bought an official one.

[img] a warehouse back office with neat shelves and a robot sorting invoices, warm small lamp in a cold room {move:up}
And the money went to the wrong places. More than half of the budgets went to sales and marketing, while the biggest returns were showing up in the boring back office.

# The turn
[img] a robot and a human sitting across a desk from each other, both looking at the same CRT screen
The five percent that worked did one thing differently. They picked narrow problems, bought tools that could learn, and let the people closest to the work decide.

[img] the same endless cubicle grid as the opening, now with a few desks lit and people sitting beside the robots {move:out}
So the takeover didn't fail because AI is useless. It stalled because replacing people turned out to be the wrong goal.

[img same] {move:hold}
And that might be the best news this story could have.
